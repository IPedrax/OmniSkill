# Internal Comms

> Status reports + FAQs · OmniSkill Operations crew

## The rules

**Lead with the conclusion.** Most readers stop after the first paragraph. If the update is "we are slipping two weeks", that is sentence one — not a reveal in paragraph four after the context.

**Bad news first, and early.** Delayed bad news is worse news, because the delay itself becomes the story and the trust cost outlasts the problem.

**Say what changed and what it means for the reader.** Everything else is optional.

## Status report

```
Status: On track / At risk / Off track
What changed since last update
What is next
What is blocked, and who can unblock it
Dates
```

Use a **consistent status vocabulary** and honour it. If "at risk" appears three weeks running and then becomes "delayed", the status label was never doing any work.

Same format, same cadence, every time — readers learn where to look and the report costs them seconds instead of minutes.

Be specific: "API integration complete, 3 of 5 endpoints tested" beats "good progress on integration".

**Never let a date slip silently.** A date that changes is the single most important thing in the update. Say the old date, the new date, and why.

## Announcement

```
What is happening
When
Who is affected
What they need to do
Where to ask questions
```

State the action required in the first two sentences. An announcement where readers must infer whether it applies to them will be ignored by the people it applies to.

For anything contentious, explain the reasoning — not to seek agreement, but because a decision without a rationale invites everyone to reconstruct one, usually less favourably than the truth.

## FAQ

Written properly, an FAQ is the highest-leverage internal document there is.

- Answer the questions people **actually ask**, in their words. Source them from the channels where they were asked.
- Include the uncomfortable ones. An FAQ that dodges the obvious question ("does this mean layoffs?") destroys trust in the whole document and everyone notices the omission.
- Answer directly in the first sentence, then elaborate.
- Say plainly when something is undecided, and when it will be decided. "We don't know yet, and we'll tell you by 15 March" is a real answer that buys goodwill; silence and vagueness do not.
- Keep it updated as new questions arrive.

## Incident comms

Different discipline: frequent, short, and honest about uncertainty.

- Acknowledge fast, even before the cause is known
- State impact, who is affected, and what is being done
- Commit to a next update time and **meet it**, even if the update is "no change yet"
- Do not speculate on cause while investigating — early wrong theories get quoted back for months
- Close the loop when resolved, and follow with `/omniskill:incident-postmortem`

## Writing

Short sentences. Plain words. No corporate abstraction — "we are reducing headcount" not "we are rightsizing our talent footprint". Everyone decodes euphemism instantly, and the attempt costs more credibility than the news.

Define acronyms on first use. Front-load: most important information first, in every section.

Scannable structure — headings, short paragraphs, bold on the load-bearing phrase.

## Match channel to message

Email for record and detail. Chat for awareness and speed. Meeting for anything with real emotional weight — significant bad news delivered by written announcement reads as avoidance.

Written follow-up after any verbal announcement, so there is a single accurate record.

## Checks before delivering

- Conclusion in the first sentence.
- Bad news is not buried.
- Reader knows whether it affects them within two sentences.
- Date changes stated explicitly with old, new, and why.
- No euphemism.
- Next update time committed where the situation is ongoing.
- Hard questions answered rather than omitted.
