# Pitch Deck

> Investor pitch decks · OmniSkill Finance crew

A deck's job is not to close the round. It is to earn the next meeting.

## The rules that govern everything

Twelve slides. One idea per slide. The headline states the point — the body proves it. If a slide needs narration to make sense, it fails when forwarded to a partner who was not in the room, which is how most decisions actually get made.

## The 12 slides

**1 — Company purpose.** One sentence. What the company does, in plain language. No adjectives, no category invention.

**2 — Problem.** Whose problem, how they solve it today, what that costs them. Specific and quantified. "The process is broken" is not a problem statement.

**3 — Solution.** What is built, and why it solves the problem stated. Show the product — a screenshot beats a paragraph.

**4 — Why now.** The strongest slide in a good deck and the missing one in most. What changed — technology, regulation, behaviour, cost curve — that makes this possible now and not three years ago? Without an answer, the implied question is why nobody has already done it.

**5 — Market size.** Bottom-up only: `customers × price × attach rate`. Top-down TAM from an analyst report signals no real thinking. Show TAM, SAM, and realistic SOM, and be honest that SOM is the number that matters.

**6 — Competition.** Name real competitors, including the incumbent and "they do nothing / use spreadsheets". A quadrant with the company alone in the top right is a tell. Show the axis on which the company genuinely wins.

**7 — Product.** How it works, briefly. Depth here belongs in the appendix.

**8 — Business model.** Price, value metric, unit economics. CAC, LTV, payback period, gross margin. See `/omniskill:pricing`.

**9 — Traction.** The slide investors read first. Revenue, growth rate, retention, engagement — whichever is genuinely strongest, on a chart with real axis labels. Pre-traction, show pipeline, pilots, or waitlist and label them as such. Never dress a pilot as revenue.

**10 — Team.** Why *these* people. Prior relevant outcomes, domain depth, the reason this founder wins this market.

**11 — Financials.** Three to five years, top-down summary. Revenue, gross margin, burn, headcount, cash-out date. From `/omniskill:3-statements`.

**12 — The ask.** Amount, runway it buys, what it is spent on (roughly: engineering / GTM / other), and the milestones it reaches. "Raising $3M for 18 months to reach $2M ARR and a Series A" is a complete ask.

## Metrics that must be honest

Investors check. Define every metric on the slide where it appears:

- **ARR** — contracted recurring revenue. Not annualised best month, not pilots, not LOIs.
- **Growth** — state the period and whether it is MoM or YoY.
- **Retention** — separate gross (churn only) from net (with expansion). Report both.
- **CAC payback** — fully loaded sales and marketing, not just ad spend.
- **Burn** — net, and give the cash-out date.

One inflated metric discovered in diligence costs the round. There is no upside to a favourable definition that will not survive a data-room review.

## Appendix

Everything cut from the main deck: cohort curves, detailed financials, product roadmap, competitive detail, customer references. Partners ask for exactly one of these; having it ready is what separates a second meeting from a delay.

## Design

Route to `/omniskill:design` for the visual system. Readable at a glance and consistent beats decorative — a deck is read on a phone as often as presented.

## Checks before delivering

- Every headline states a claim, not a topic.
- Market size is bottom-up.
- "Why now" has a real answer.
- Every metric is defined and would survive diligence.
- The ask names an amount, a runway, and a milestone.
